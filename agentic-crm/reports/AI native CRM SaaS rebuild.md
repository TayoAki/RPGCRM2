# Turn Magpie's Single-Tenant Cells Into SaaS

Magpie is not a SaaS today. It is single-tenant software that a sign-up page installs automatically: every customer gets a separate Railway project with one app container and one Postgres database, the code assumes one company per process ("One instance per customer ... no multi-tenant code"), and the control plane is a roughly 1,640-line provisioner with one table and no billing, usage metering, limits, suspension, deletion, backups or operator console ([PLAN.md](../PLAN.md), [provision.ts](../platform/src/provision.ts), [COGS.md](../COGS.md)). It also copies the operator's vendor keys into every workspace, which leaves one CopilotKit key able to read every customer's Bot threads and most likely puts Magpie outside the internal-use terms of its contact-data vendors ([platform/README.md](../platform/README.md), [Apollo API terms](https://www.apollo.io/terms/api)). The AI-native CRMs that now set buyer expectations, led by Attio, Lightfield and Day AI with Clarify, Zero and Rox close behind, all run one pooled store keyed by workspace, build the customer record from email, calendar and meetings without anyone typing, enrich records automatically, and meter AI with visible credits. Feature by feature, Magpie leads on governed Bots that act under field-level permissions, on reaching the owner over Telegram and SMS, and on a vertical GovCon edition backed by live public data; it trails on automatic capture, enrichment of existing records, an MCP server, Slack, mobile and SOC 2. The recommended plan runs in four phases over about nine months: make today's cells sellable and legal in six weeks, retrofit pooled tenancy with Postgres row-level security and a durable tenant-fair agent runtime, build capture on Gmail, Calendar and Outlook once Google's CASA assessment clears, then earn SOC 2 and sign enrichment resale terms. Cash outside salaries is modest, roughly $30,000 to $70,000 in year one with SOC 2 the largest line; the real cost is an estimated 30 to 46 engineer-weeks, and the payoff is that Magpie finally competes on the data capture buyers treat as table stakes while hosting falls from $5 to $12 per customer to an estimated $0.30 to $1.50.

## Magpie is single-tenant hosting with a self-serve front door

Each Magpie customer is a whole deployment. A sign-up makes the platform service create a Railway project named `magpie-<slug>` containing a pgvector Postgres container with its own volume and one app container running the shared `magpie-crm` image, then hand the owner a set-password link ([provision.ts](../platform/src/provision.ts), [railway.ts](../platform/src/railway.ts)). Inside that container s6 supervises the API, the routine worker, start-up migrations and, by default, the Bot's Playwright browser ([Dockerfile](../deploy/railway/Dockerfile)). The design is deliberate: PLAN.md's architecture table reads "**One instance per customer** | Simple isolation, no multi-tenant code" ([PLAN.md](../PLAN.md)). The code bears that out. **None of the roughly 125 tables (95 in OpenDuet's schema, 30 in the CRM schema) carries a tenant column**; `crm.configuration` is a single row with id 1, `users.email` is globally unique, custom objects are keyed by name alone, a 1,594-line config file reads keys and budgets from environment variables once at boot, branding is baked in when the image is built, and the mail, IMAP, workflow and routine loops each scan the one database they started against ([core.ts](../openduet/openbot/server/src/db/schema/core.ts), [crm/schema.ts](../openduet/openbot/server/src/crm/schema.ts), [config.ts](../openduet/openbot/server/src/config.ts), [services.ts](../openduet/openbot/server/src/crm/services.ts), [README.md](../README.md)).

In Microsoft's taxonomy this is an "automated single-tenant deployment," the model where "if a single tenant requires a specific infrastructure cost, 100 tenants probably require 100 times that cost" ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/guide/multitenant/considerations/tenancy-models)). That is not wrong in itself. HubSpot runs independent copies of its MySQL stack per region, which it calls Hublets ([HubSpot](https://product.hubspot.com/blog/our-journey-to-multiregion-vtickets)), and Magpie's own pitch leans on isolation: "Each company gets its own isolated project and database ... Nothing is shared between companies" ([AI_TEAMS_PLAN.md](../AI_TEAMS_PLAN.md)). Hosting is also not where the money goes. A workspace costs **$5 a month idle and $8 to $12 in use**, while AI is **about 85% of a typical seat's $26.30 monthly cost** ([COGS.md](../COGS.md)). The problem is everything a business needs around the cells, and almost none of it exists:

| What a SaaS back office does | Magpie today | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Self-serve sign-up and provisioning | Works, resumes after restarts, capped at 50 workspaces by default | [app.ts](../platform/src/app.ts), [config.ts](../platform/src/config.ts) |
| Ship releases to customers | One cell at a time, only when the sign-up service starts; no canary or retry | [index.ts](../platform/src/index.ts), [provision.ts](../platform/src/provision.ts) |
| Plans, subscriptions, invoices | None; "bill through Stripe outside the product" | [FEATURES.md](../FEATURES.md), [COGS.md](../COGS.md) |
| Usage metering and limits | "None of these are enforced yet"; `model_usage` has no token or cost columns | [COGS.md](../COGS.md), [admin.ts](../openduet/openbot/server/src/db/schema/admin.ts) |
| Suspend, delete, expire trials | No Railway delete or pause call exists | [railway.ts](../platform/src/railway.ts) |
| Backups and restore | Nothing automated on Railway; a manual `pg_dump` for self-hosted | [README.md](../README.md) |
| Operator console and support access | None | [APP_MAP.md](../APP_MAP.md) |
| Let customers connect Gmail | "Coming soon," blocked on an OAuth relay and Google's CASA review | [OAUTH_RELAY.md](../platform/OAUTH_RELAY.md) |

The cells are also less isolated than the pitch says. The sign-up service copies the operator's OpenAI, CopilotKit Intelligence, CopilotKit license, Composio and treg keys into every workspace ([config.ts](../platform/src/config.ts)). The platform README concedes that separation in Intelligence "is kept by the app, not by Intelligence: **every workspace's server holds a key that can read the whole project**," so one compromised workspace exposes every customer's Bot conversations ([platform/README.md](../platform/README.md)). Composio connections are filed under the bare user id with no workspace prefix ([shared-accounts.ts](../openduet/openbot/server/src/plugins/shared-accounts.ts)), treg lead lookups carry no workspace identifier ([treg.ts](../openduet/openbot/server/src/crm/prospecting/treg.ts)), and no vendor bill can be split by customer. Intelligence is also the biggest pricing unknown: the $49 plan covers 10,000 interactions across all customers, which COGS.md estimates is **only about 40 typical seats** if one interaction is one run ([COGS.md](../COGS.md)).

So Magpie passes the self-serve test and fails the run-a-business test. A stranger can sign up and get a working CRM, but Magpie cannot charge them, see what they cost, stop them at a limit, pause them when a card fails, delete them when a trial lapses, restore their data, or let them connect Gmail. The ceilings are close, too: the 50-workspace default, and Railway Pro's limit of **100 projects per workspace**, which means customer 101 needs a second Railway account or an Enterprise contract ([Railway pricing](https://railway.com/pricing)).

## AI-native rivals build the record themselves and meter the agents

**Attio** now calls itself "The CRM for agentic revenue" ([Attio](https://attio.com/)). Its model is objects, records, attributes and lists, with formula attributes since July 2026, and its email and calendar sync creates people and companies by default from Gmail or Outlook, at a claimed 15 million emails a day ([Attio docs](https://docs.attio.com/docs/objects-and-lists.md), [Attio help](https://attio.com/help/reference/email-calendar/email-and-calendar-syncing), [Attio](https://attio.com/)). Built-in enrichment comes from unnamed third parties, and "**it is not possible to export enriched data from Attio**," a strong hint that the data is licensed for display only ([Attio help](https://attio.com/help/reference/managing-your-data/enriched-data)). The AI layer is Ask Attio (a chat agent that can act), AI attributes, agent blocks inside Workflows rebuilt in June 2026, a hosted MCP server with 48 tools, and read-only SQL ([changelog](https://attio.com/changelog/2026/the-new-workflows), [MCP docs](https://docs.attio.com/mcp/overview.md)). Everything AI draws on seat and workspace credit pools, with packs priced at roughly **$0.0095 to $0.014 a credit** and a daily briefing costing 30 to 50 credits ([Attio credits](https://attio.com/help/reference/workspace-settings-billing/manage-workspace-and-seat-credits), [Attio pricing](https://attio.com/pricing)). Pro is $100 a month per workspace with one user plus $79 per extra user on annual billing. Attio claims 30,000+ customers, though its last clean paid figure was 5,000 in August 2025 ([Attio pricing](https://attio.com/pricing), [Attio](https://attio.com/), [Series B](https://attio.com/blog/attio-raises-52m-series-b)).

**Lightfield**, the re-founded Tome team, raised a $47 million a16z-led Series A in September 2026 with about 40 staff ([Upstarts Media](https://www.upstartsmedia.com/p/ai-sales-startup-lightfield-raises-47m)). It builds accounts and contacts from Google or Outlook mail and calendar, a native recorder, Slack, Teams, WhatsApp and LinkedIn DMs, and it "**never creates opportunities on its own** from email or meetings" ([Lightfield docs](https://docs.lightfield.app/help/settings/mail-and-calendar/how-records-are-created/), [integrations](https://docs.lightfield.app/help/settings/integrations/)). Every field has an AI fill setting of Off, Suggest or Auto, and enrichment has the same per-field policy plus a paid advanced tier for phones and verified emails ([data model](https://docs.lightfield.app/help/settings/data-model/), [usage](https://docs.lightfield.app/help/settings/usage/)). Drafts from automations wait in a For review queue, and chat edits default to confirmation cards ([For review](https://docs.lightfield.app/help/for-review/), [agent settings](https://docs.lightfield.app/help/settings/agent/)). Pricing is the most instructive part: CEO Keith Peiris says pure seat pricing failed because "the head was using 10,000X more than the tail," pure consumption credits produced "the worst three weeks of the company's life," and the result is a hybrid of platform fee plus metered work: "**We have to charge for the work**" ([a16z Show](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/the-a16z-show/the-ai-native-crm)). Starter is $89 per user for up to three users; Pro is $999 a month per workspace with five capture seats, unlimited viewers and an annual credit pool ([Lightfield pricing](https://lightfield.com/pricing)).

**Day AI**, founded by HubSpot's former product chief and backed by about $24 million in Sequoia-led rounds ([Upstarts Media](https://www.upstartsmedia.com/p/day-ai-sequoia-ai-crm)), has moved from "AI-native CRM" to "context graph and agent control plane" that runs "in place of Salesforce or HubSpot or as a layer on top of them" ([Day AI llms.txt](https://day.ai/llms.txt), [Series A](https://www.day.ai/resources/series-a-and-the-beginning-of-the-shift-in-crm)). It ingests Gmail, Calendar, a meeting bot and Slack natively, reaches Microsoft 365 through MCP connectors, and reads and writes Salesforce or HubSpot through those vendors' own MCP servers under each user's login ([Day AI vs Lightfield](https://day.ai/guides/day-ai-vs-lightfield), [Microsoft update](https://day.ai/resources/microsoft-users-can-now-use-day-ai), [security review](https://day.ai/guides/ai-crm-security-review)). Agents are named per person and inherit that person's permissions; every tool is set to Always allow, Needs approval or Off, and skills can run in a "learning mode" that reports what they would have done ([control access](https://day.ai/guides/control-ai-agents-crm-access)). It prices **per agent, not per human**: $24, $60 or $200 a month on annual billing, with teammates free and no usage fees, so interactive chat is what its CEO calls an "LLM open bar" ([Day AI pricing](https://day.ai/pricing), [Upstarts Media](https://www.upstartsmedia.com/p/day-ai-sequoia-ai-crm)).

The wider field has converged. Clarify sells unlimited seats with a credit pool and never gates recording ([Clarify pricing](https://www.clarify.ai/pricing)); Zero states the rule most vendors now follow, that internal, reversible writes are autonomous while anything that "leaves your workspace and reaches a customer" needs approval ([Zero](https://zero.inc/blog/ai-agents-crm-guardrails)); Rox runs agent swarms over the customer's CRM and warehouse with a 15-provider enrichment waterfall ([Rox pricing](https://www.rox.com/pricing)); and incumbents meter per action, at $0.10 a standard Agentforce action and $0.50 per resolved HubSpot conversation ([Salesforce](https://www.salesforce.com/agentforce/pricing/), [HubSpot](https://www.hubspot.com/company-news/hubspots-customer-agent-and-prospecting-agent-now-you-pay-when-the-task-is-complete)). The loudest buyer complaints are unpredictable credit burn and autonomous outbound that spams; early AI SDR products saw heavy churn and buyers moved to "AI drafts, human sends" ([eesel.ai](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/is-hubspot-breeze-worth-it), [Autobound](https://autobound.ai/blog/ai-sdr-buying-guide-2026)). The pattern is lopsided. Magpie is ahead where competitors are cautious: its Bots act under the same field-level permissions and audit trail as people, its Bot teams that take weekly jobs from a company brief go further than most rivals, and no notable competitor reviewed offers Telegram or SMS as a channel to the agent, where the norm is Slack, as with Rox's Slack DMs ([Rox Slack docs](https://docs.rox.com/development/engineering/docs/rox-enterprise-integrations/slack-integration)). That last point is an inference from absence, but it is a real advantage for owner-operators who live on their phones. Its GovCon edition is exactly the kind of proprietary data others are buying: Clarify acquired Seam for datasets "that can't be reached with a simple search," and Zoom agreed to buy Common Room for its buyer signals ([IndustryLens](https://industry-lens.com/reports/signal-spotlight-clarify), [Zoom IR](https://investors.zoom.us/news-releases/news-release-details/zoom-acquire-common-room-bringing-buyer-intelligence-its-ai)). But Magpie is behind on the one thing every rival treats as the foundation: **the record fills itself**. In Attio, Lightfield and Day AI a new contact, its emails, its meetings and its company appear without anyone asking; in Magpie they appear only if a Bot is told to log them. One Magpie choice also runs against the market: asking before every CRM write is stricter than the "internal writes are autonomous" norm, and it will read as friction once capture produces hundreds of small updates a day.

## The leaders run pooled stores, fair queues and a metered model gateway

**Attio built heavy, custom infrastructure** that a small team should not copy, but its habits are worth stealing. It runs on Google Cloud with Cloud Spanner as its primary database, a Rust write path for its "Particle" graph-relational model that emits change events through Pub/Sub, TypeScript everywhere else, and a Postgres-compatible SQL engine it wrote from scratch ([formula attributes](https://attio.com/engineering/blog/how-we-built-formula-attributes), [new architecture](https://attio.com/engineering/blog/A-new-type-of-user-requires-a-new-type-of-architecture)). Three habits transfer. Background work gets a Pub/Sub ordering key per workspace, so one tenant is served by a slice of the worker pool and cannot starve others. Its agent framework runs the same code whether a task takes "4 seconds or 48 hours," and **every model and tool call is "attributed to the workspace, the feature, and the request"** ([Thread Agent](https://attio.com/engineering/blog/you-cant-just-prompt-your-way-to-great-ai-features)). And Ask Attio builds its tool registry fresh on each turn from the user's permissions and feature flags, with automatic failover across Anthropic, Vertex AI and OpenAI ([Ask Attio](https://attio.com/engineering/blog/ask-attio-a-technical-look-at-our-new-agent)). Its MCP server uses OAuth with the user's own login, auto-approves reads, confirms writes, and offers "fewer, richer tools" designed for agents rather than wrappers around the API ([Building the MCP server](https://attio.com/engineering/blog/building-the-attio-mcp-server)).

**Lightfield proves a plain stack goes very far.** Its job posts describe "a deliberately simple stack: Postgres as the system of record, a sharded transactional outbox for change events, Redis-buffered sync into Typesense for search, BullMQ for processing, and Postgres-backed customer-facing analytics with **per-organization row-level security**" ([Lightfield data infrastructure job](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/Lightfield/094fac84-e417-4fd7-b9fd-49d7efec8a2e)). That stack serves **5 billion queries a month and 10 million background jobs a day across about 170 queues** ([Lightfield backend job](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/Lightfield/46e9843d-3b60-4f54-b4f8-ae8784559383)). The data model is entity-attribute-value with typed edges and versioned values, and it started as a Facebook-style activity log: raw interactions live in the log, and fields such as deal stage are derived from it and can be refilled later ([a16z Show](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/the-a16z-show/the-ai-native-crm)). Lightfield built its own email and Slack sync. Its current hiring lists what turned out to be hard: record-level permissions everywhere, durable work with retries and versioning, and "metering primitives for consumption limits and billing." Magpie should plan those from day one rather than discover them.

**Day AI is the right template for Magpie's size.** It says "We're small, and we want to stay that way," has no open engineering roles, and assembled its product from managed services visible in its subprocessor list: Supabase and PlanetScale for databases, turbopuffer for search, ClickHouse for analytics, Inngest for durable workflows, E2B for code execution, Anthropic, OpenAI, Baseten and Fireworks for models, Braintrust for monitoring, AssemblyAI and Hyperdoc for meetings, and Parallel, Exa and Perplexity for web enrichment ([Day AI careers](https://day.ai/careers), [subprocessors](https://day.ai/trust/subprocessors)). What it builds itself is the part that matters: a typed graph where every value carries its source and history, custom properties whose description "is actually instructions to an llm on how to fill out the property," and a 78-tool MCP server that doubles as its API ([SCHEMA.md](https://github.com/day-ai/day-ai-sdk/blob/master/SCHEMA.md), [TOOLS.md](https://github.com/day-ai/day-ai-sdk/blob/master/TOOLS.md)). Its email privacy rule is worth copying verbatim: one private setting from any teammate keeps a whole thread private, and new accounts start with email private ([security review](https://day.ai/guides/ai-crm-security-review)).

On tenancy, the evidence points one way. Attio keys its background work and rate limits by workspace on shared infrastructure, Lightfield's is pooled Postgres with per-organization security, and Day AI's vendor set implies a pooled store; none is described as a database per customer (the Lightfield and Day AI readings are inferences from job posts and vendor lists). Twenty, the open-source CRM, uses a Postgres schema per workspace, which forces every migration to run once per tenant, and Neon calls schema-per-user "generally not recommended" for SaaS ([Twenty source](https://github.com/twentyhq/twenty/tree/main/packages/twenty-server/src/engine/workspace-datasource), [Neon](https://neon.com/docs/guides/multitenancy)). Microsoft's recommended middle path, "vertically partitioned," puts most tenants on shared infrastructure and sells dedicated deployments to customers who need them, noting "you can even charge customers a higher rate" ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/guide/multitenant/considerations/tenancy-models)). For Magpie, that turns today's cells into a premium tier instead of the default cost structure. What to borrow, in one place:

| Pattern | Who does it | Magpie's version |
|---|---|---|
| Every row keyed by tenant, enforced by the database | Lightfield, Attio | `tenant_id` plus forced row-level security on all ~125 tables |
| Per-tenant fairness in background work | Attio (ordering key per workspace) | Bot runs and routines queued with the tenant as concurrency key |
| Every model call attributed to tenant, feature and request | Attio | A usage ledger with tokens and cost that feeds billing |
| Raw interactions kept as linked objects, fields derived from them | Lightfield, Day AI | An activities table plus field values that store source, time and fill policy |
| Off, Suggest or Auto per field | Lightfield | One policy shared by AI fill and enrichment |
| Always allow, Needs approval, Off per tool, plus learning mode | Day AI | Replaces "ask before every write" |
| No automatic deals; a guardrail before closed-won | Lightfield, Day AI | Bots propose deals and never close them |
| Agent-shaped MCP with OAuth and the user's permissions | Attio, Day AI | MCP over Magpie's existing records layer |
| Two model providers behind one gateway with failover | Attio, Day AI, Lightfield | OpenAI plus Anthropic through one proxy |

Magpie already has more of this than it looks. All CRM reads and writes pass through one records layer that applies the user's role and field rules, field-level change history exists, and Bots call tools under the user's role ([FEATURES.md](../FEATURES.md)). The gap is that OpenDuet's own stores (routines, approvals, memory, delivery) bypass that layer, and nothing below it knows about tenants.

## Enrichment is a licensing problem before it is a data problem

The data itself is cheap. At small volume a verified work email costs about **$0.009 to $0.02** from LeadMagic or Hunter and about $0.05 from waterfall aggregators like FullEnrich; a mobile number costs $0.04 to $0.55; verification costs $0.004 to $0.02; a full person or company record costs $0.03 to $0.28; and AI search calls from Exa, Parallel or Firecrawl cost a tenth of a cent to a cent ([LeadMagic](https://leadmagic.io/pricing), [Hunter](https://hunter.io/pricing), [FullEnrich](https://fullenrich.com/pricing), [ZeroBounce](https://www.zerobounce.net/email-validation-pricing), [PDL](https://www.peopledatalabs.com/pricing), [Exa](https://exa.ai/pricing), [Parallel](https://docs.parallel.ai/getting-started/pricing.md)). A waterfall of LeadMagic first, FullEnrich on misses and MillionVerifier on everything works out to roughly **$0.038 per attempted contact** at entry plans and about $0.02 at volume, assuming a 60% first-pass hit rate that a 200 to 500 contact bake-off should confirm ([MillionVerifier pricing](https://puzzleinbox.com/blog/millionverifier-pricing-guide)). Phones dominate cost everywhere, so fetch them only when a person asks.

The rights are the hard part. Apollo's API terms forbid sublicensing, selling or distributing its APIs ([Apollo API terms](https://www.apollo.io/terms/api)), and the Apollo schedule that flows through Firecrawl forbids sharing the data "outside your organization, including through products, datasets, APIs, exports, or **agent outputs visible to outsiders**" ([Firecrawl Apollo schedule](https://www.firecrawl.dev/terms/third-party-data/apollo/F-1.0.2)). Magpie's prospecting serves Hunter and Apollo results to every tenant through the operator's shared treg key ([treg.ts](../openduet/openbot/server/src/crm/prospecting/treg.ts)); each tenant is an outsider to the operator's organization, so this is very likely outside internal-use terms and is the top licensing risk to fix. Explorium, which sells licensed data, describes four tiers (internal use, display, redistribution, full resale) and says OEM licenses "often require $50K+ minimums" and six to twelve months of negotiation ([Explorium](https://www.explorium.ai/blog/data-for-gtm/b2b-data-api-resale-rights-licensing-what-product-builders-need-to-know-now-in-2026.md)). Two routes are safe for a small multi-tenant product. The first is **bring your own key**, where the tenant holds the vendor contract and Magpie only processes. The second is a vendor with published embed terms: FullEnrich's Enterprise tier offers a reseller agreement with sub-accounts, Findymail says its "credit-based plans are for reselling or embedding data," and Apollo's Data Reseller program says most partners sign "within a week" ([FullEnrich](https://fullenrich.com/pricing), [Findymail](https://www.findymail.com/pricing/), [Apollo reseller](https://www.apollo.io/partners/api-reseller)).

Compliance tightens the same screws. LinkedIn keeps winning against scrapers that use fake or logged-in accounts: hiQ ended in a $500,000 consent judgment and an injunction, Proxycurl shut down in July 2025 to comply with its settlement, and ProAPIs accepted a consent judgment in September 2026 ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/blogs/sourcingatmorganlewis/2022/12/linkedin-v-hiq-landmark-data-scraping-suit-provides-guidance-to-data-scrapers-and-web-operators), [Nubela](https://nubela.co/blog/goodbye-proxycurl/), [The Record](https://therecord.media/linkedin-wins-court-order-blocking-mass-scraping)). France's CNIL fined Kaspr €240,000 for collecting contacts LinkedIn users had hidden and ruled that saying data came "from publicly accessible sources" does not answer an access request ([CNIL](https://www.cnil.fr/en/data-scraping-kaspr-fined-eu240000)). Since August 2026, California requires registered data brokers to process the state's deletion feed every 45 days ([Fenwick](https://www.fenwick.com/insights/publication/dont-drop-ball-upcoming-changes-california-delete-act-five-key-steps-companies)). For Magpie that means four things: confirm how the LinkedIn engager source collects its data, and drop it if it uses fake or pooled accounts; store the provider and date on every enriched field so access requests can name sources; keep enrichment caches per tenant, because a shared cross-tenant people cache starts to look like a data broker; and store only Google `place_id` values from Places, since other fields have no caching exception ([OpenPlacesAPI](https://openplacesapi.com/blog/can-you-store-places-api-results)).

None of the leaders owns a contact database. Lightfield and Day AI lean on first-party capture plus third-party lookups, and Day AI's enrichment subprocessors are Parallel, Exa and Perplexity, the same web-research layer Magpie already uses ([Day AI subprocessors](https://day.ai/trust/subprocessors)). The lesson is to **build the router, cache, meter and compliance plumbing, and buy the data**; Magpie's real data moat is its vertical engines like Kontract, not contact lists.

## Conclusion: rebuild in four phases over about nine months

The case for pooling Magpie is not hosting cost, since AI is 85% of a seat whatever the tenancy model. It is the three things a fleet of cells cannot do cheaply: run one Google OAuth client and one mail-sync service for every customer, fit inside one SOC 2 scope, and grow past 100 tenants without a person babysitting rollouts. Retrofitting the existing code beats a greenfield rewrite, because Magpie's reasons to buy (governed Bots, out-of-app channels, GovCon) already live in it, and Lightfield shows a plain Postgres-and-queue stack scales well beyond Magpie's horizon. Keeping cells as a paid dedicated tier turns the isolation pitch into a premium product instead of a tax on every customer. The sequence matters: the control plane comes first because billing, metering, limits and lifecycle carry over unchanged into the pooled world, and capture comes last because Google's assessment clock, not engineering, sets its date.

### Phase 1, November to December 2026: make the cells sellable and legal

Turn the `platform.workspaces` table into a tenant registry holding plan, status (trial, active, past due, suspended, deleted) and which deployment serves the tenant, the "deployment stamp" map Microsoft recommends so a tenant can later move from a cell into the pool without changing its account ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/guide/multitenant/considerations/tenancy-models)). Bill through **Stripe Billing**, which costs 0.7% of billing volume on top of card fees and includes a Meters API with up to 100 million events a month, enough to meter runs without a second vendor ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/billing/pricing)). Record tokens and cost on every model call against workspace, Bot and feature, set OpenAI's `prompt_cache_key` per workspace so cache accounting stays separate, and run COGS.md's one-week measurement to replace the $0.10-per-run guess ([OpenAI prompt caching](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-caching), [COGS.md](../COGS.md)). Enforce limits in COGS.md's order (runs a month, steps a run, routine frequency) with its 80%, 100% and 120% thresholds. Add the missing Railway delete and pause calls, trial expiry, a nightly `pg_dump` per cell with a tested restore, a small operator console with audited impersonation, and staged rollouts with retries. Fix vendor isolation now: give paying customers their own Intelligence project or decide to replace it, prefix Composio user ids with the tenant, and tag every vendor call. Switch Apollo and Hunter to bring-your-own-key, confirm the LinkedIn source, and confirm whether customer cells run the Bot browser, since the image turns it on by default and the provisioner never turns it off ([provision.ts](../platform/src/provision.ts)). Finally, build the OAuth relay and **start Google's CASA assessment immediately**: restricted Gmail scopes such as `gmail.readonly` need a lab-run Assurance Level 1 assessment renewed yearly, the self-scan path is deprecated, and unverified apps are capped at 100 new users for life ([Google](https://developers.google.com/identity/protocols/oauth2/production-readiness/restricted-scope-verification), [GitHub issue quoting Google](https://github.com/MehdiMamas/keytray/issues/26)). Ship `gmail.send` first, which is a sensitive scope needing verification but no CASA ([DeepStrike](https://deepstrike.io/blog/google-casa-security-assessment-2025)).

### Phase 2, January to March 2027: pool the data and own the agent runtime

Move standard-tier tenants into one Postgres on **Neon**, which charges $0.106 per compute-hour and $0.35 per GB-month with no project fee, and keep a Neon project per tenant for the dedicated tier, as Neon itself recommends ([Neon pricing](https://neon.com/pricing), [Neon guide](https://neon.com/docs/guides/multitenancy)). Add `tenant_id` to every table with forced row-level security, connect as a non-owner role, and set the tenant with a transaction-local `set_config` so it cannot leak through a connection pooler ([AWS](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/database/multi-tenant-data-isolation-with-postgresql-row-level-security/)). A design point of my own, not from a source: give the column a default of `current_setting('app.tenant_id')`, and most existing Drizzle queries need no edits, because inserts fill the tenant and the policy filters reads. The real work is the singletons and unique keys (users become global identities with memberships through Better Auth's organization plugin, objects key on tenant plus name, configuration keys on tenant), moving per-tenant keys and budgets out of the boot-time config into an encrypted settings table, branding per request, a shared rate limiter in place of the in-memory one, and isolation tests in CI. Replace the `setInterval` loops and the 30-second routine worker with a durable engine: **DBOS** keeps workflow state as rows in the same Postgres for free, so row-level security and backups cover it, while **Trigger.dev Pro** at $50 a month gives each tenant its own concurrency pool and stops counting waits against it ([DBOS](https://www.dbos.dev/pricing), [Trigger.dev](https://trigger.dev/docs/queue-concurrency)). Use one schedule per routine type that fans out per tenant, not one schedule per tenant, and make approvals durable waits. Put **LiteLLM** in front of OpenAI and Anthropic for per-tenant budgets, spend tracking and failover at no license cost ([DEV](https://dev.to/marcuswwchen/virtual-keys-per-tenant-ditching-our-custom-llm-billing-layer-2p7b)). If the CopilotKit quote does not give enforced per-tenant isolation at a sane price, move threads and memory into Magpie's own tables. Run one platform Telegram bot with deep-link account linking, which keeps a single webhook under Telegram's ~30 messages a second limit, and offer a branded bot on Business ([Telegram FAQ](https://core.telegram.org/bots/faq)). Fork OpenDuet for good: Magpie already edits it in place and syncs it by hand about monthly, and upstream assumes one tenant ([README.md](../README.md)).

### Phase 3, February to May 2027: capture mail, calendar and meetings

Build Gmail and Google Calendar sync in-house, as Lightfield did, because capture is the product: Gmail push through Pub/Sub, a watch renewed daily (Google requires at least every 7 days), catch-up through `history.list`, and a periodic fallback because notifications can be dropped ([Gmail push](https://developers.google.com/workspace/gmail/api/guides/push)). Add Outlook through Microsoft Graph after publisher verification, which is free but needs a Microsoft partner account ([Microsoft](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/entra/identity-platform/publisher-verification-overview)); if that slips, Nylas at about $1.50 to $2 per connected account is the fallback ([Nylas](https://cli.nylas.com/guides/email-api-pricing-models)). Create people and companies with Lightfield's defaults (only when someone replies or you reach out, filtering automated and personal-domain mail), never create deals, store every email, meeting and message as an activity linked to records, and apply Day AI's "most private setting wins" rule. Give every field an Off, Suggest or Auto fill policy with a stored source per value, run enrichment on record creation through the router, and buy transcription rather than build it (Day AI uses AssemblyAI; its price was not researched here). Ship an MCP server over the records layer with OAuth, the user's permissions, automatic reads and confirmed writes, and replace "ask before every write" with per-tool Always allow, Needs approval or Off plus Day AI's learning mode, keeping approval mandatory for anything that reaches a customer. Turn on `gmail.readonly` for everyone once the CASA letter arrives; one company went from evidence to approval in about 30 days ([CellCog](https://cellcog.ai/blog/casa-security-assessment/)).

### Phase 4, April to September 2027: earn trust and sell data

Start SOC 2 with Type I, then a Type II observation window; a compliance platform runs about $7,500 to $15,000 a year and a first Type II audit $15,000 to $45,000, and pooling shrinks the audit to one environment ([Techplained](https://www.techplained.com/best-soc2-compliance-tools), [Agency](https://blog.getagency.com/articles/soc-2-audit-cost-for-startups)). Publish a DPA and subprocessor list, which GDPR Article 28 effectively requires, and a no-training commitment like the ones Attio, Lightfield and Day AI publish ([GDPR Art. 28](https://gdpr-info.eu/art-28-gdpr/), [Attio security](https://attio.com/help/reference/workspace-settings-billing/security/security-at-attio), [Lightfield privacy](https://lightfield.com/privacy), [Day AI data policy](https://day.ai/data)). Build the suppression list, deletion propagation from vendor notices and access requests, and retention limits on unused enriched contacts. Sign FullEnrich Enterprise or Apollo's reseller agreement before selling bundled enrichment credits, and defer $50,000-plus OEM licenses until volume justifies them. Get 10 active Slack installs, then submit to the Marketplace, whose review takes up to 10 weeks; non-Marketplace apps are limited to one history read a minute ([Slack review guide](https://docs.slack.dev/slack-marketplace/slack-marketplace-review-guide), [Slack rate limits](https://docs.slack.dev/changelog/2025/05/29/rate-limit-changes-for-non-marketplace-apps)). Offer SMS as a Business add-on, since Twilio registers each customer as a separate brand at roughly $20 to $61 once plus $1.50 to $10 a month and about a week of approval ([Twilio](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/phone-numbers/a2p-10dlc), [Twilio fees](https://support.twilio.com/hc/en-us/articles/1260803965530-Pricing-and-Fees-for-A2P-10DLC-Service)). Optionally, copy Day AI's other door: sell Magpie Bots on top of a customer's HubSpot or Salesforce through those vendors' MCP servers, for buyers who will not rip out their CRM.

### Price working seats and runs, give viewers away

Keep the shape COGS.md already chose, a price per seat with a pooled run allowance, because it matches where Lightfield landed after both pure models failed. Keep **Team at $99 and Business at $199 per working seat** with 400 and 800 pooled runs, which COGS.md puts at 70% and 78% typical margin ([COGS.md](../COGS.md)). Three changes follow from the research. Make viewers free (people who read and comment but have no Bot of their own), as Lightfield, Day AI, Clarify and Rox all do in some form, because Magpie's cost lives in runs, not logins ([Lightfield pricing](https://lightfield.com/pricing), [Day AI pricing](https://day.ai/pricing), [Clarify pricing](https://www.clarify.ai/pricing), [Rox pricing](https://www.rox.com/pricing)). Keep capture unmetered, as Lightfield and Clarify do, but cap recording minutes per seat ([Lightfield usage](https://docs.lightfield.app/help/settings/usage/)). And once pooling removes the $5 to $12 cell floor, add a **Solo plan near $49 with about 150 runs**; at $0.10 a run, under $1.50 of hosting and Stripe's fee, it clears roughly 45% to 60% margin depending on what Composio really costs. Sell run packs as planned, but test a higher price: Attio's list price for a daily briefing works out to roughly $0.29 to $0.70, against Magpie's $0.15 a run ([Attio credits](https://attio.com/help/reference/workspace-settings-billing/manage-workspace-and-seat-credits)). Price bundled enrichment at about $0.10 per verified contact once resale terms are signed (roughly 2.5 times the waterfall cost) and keep bring-your-own-key free. Before publishing, verify two inputs that could move margin: Composio's list price is $18 a seat with 10,000 calls a seat included, then **$0.005 per call**, against the ~$0.0003 COGS.md assumes, which at ~1,600 calls per person is about $8 a person instead of ~$1 ([Composio](https://composio.dev/pricing)), and the `gpt-5.6-terra` price, which is unpublished.
